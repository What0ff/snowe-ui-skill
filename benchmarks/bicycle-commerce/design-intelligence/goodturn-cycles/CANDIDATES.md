# Architecture and Art-Direction Candidates

## Experience architecture

### A — Catalog showroom

Start with filterable bikes, then product pages and checkout. Strong for returning riders and immediate price visibility; weak for novices because component/category language carries the selection burden.

### B — Obligatory guided finder

Start with a short questionnaire, then reveal one recommended bike and alternatives. Strong for reassurance; rejected because the entire three-bike offer and prices become hidden behind interaction.

### C — City-use product field — selected

Show all three bikes as distinct daily-use answers, then let visitors filter, compare, or open an optional guide. Embed fit, ownership terms, workshop care, test rides, and reservation at the decision points. This is structurally neither a mass catalog nor a landing-page story: the assortment itself is the navigation field.

### D — One-day editorial route

Tell a morning-to-evening city story and introduce bikes along the route. Most expressive but slowest for direct product comparison; useful as campaign/editorial content, not the commerce spine.

## Art direction

### 1 — Precision gallery

White/black manufacturer-like stage, oversized bike photography, minimal technical labeling. Makes product form clear but risks looking like a generic premium DTC manufacturer and underplays independent service.

### 2 — City atlas

Saturated map colors, route modules, playful illustrated neighborhoods. Ownable and energetic, but the information system competes with bike truth and can resemble public-transport branding.

### 3 — Workshop field guide — selected

Warm paper, mechanical ink, vermilion/citron signals, condensed display type, ruled fit/route marks, and grounded photography. It can hold product desire, fitting precision, and service warmth in one thesis; the signature linework is limited to orientation and service proof.
